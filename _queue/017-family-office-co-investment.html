What a family office is

A family office manages the wealth of one family (a single-family office) or several (a multi-family office). Its job covers investments, and often tax, succession, philanthropy and administration as well. There is no single legal form or regulatory category. Some are a handful of people running a portfolio. Others employ dozens of investment professionals and behave much like a private equity firm.

What sets them apart from pension funds and insurers is the source of the money. A family office answers to the family, not to outside investors. That means fewer rules about what it may buy, a longer time horizon, and decisions that often depend on a small number of people.

How family offices invest in private deals

  • Fund commitments. Most family offices still invest in private equity, venture, credit and infrastructure through funds managed by others. This gives diversification and access, at the cost of fees and less control.
  • Co-investment. A fund or sponsor leading a deal offers a share of it to selected investors, usually its own fund investors, often at reduced or no management fee and carried interest. The family office gets exposure to a specific company or asset, with the lead doing the work.
  • Direct investment. The family office finds, assesses and negotiates a deal itself, sometimes taking a board seat or control. This needs an in-house team and the appetite for concentrated risk.
  • Club deals. Several families pool capital for a deal too large or too specialised for one of them. One family or an adviser usually leads.

Large projects: infrastructure, real estate and energy

Family offices are a natural fit for assets with long lives and steady cash flows, such as renewable energy, data centres, logistics property, healthcare facilities and transport. Their patience suits projects that take years to build and decades to pay back. In practice they rarely fund a large project alone. They typically take part of the equity, alongside a sponsor, infrastructure funds or strategic investors, while banks or bond investors provide the debt. What family offices look for

  1. Alignment. Sponsors and founders with their own money in the deal, and a fair split of returns and control.
  2. A clear thesis. Why this asset, why now, and what has to go right. One page that a principal can read in five minutes.
  3. Governance and information rights. Board seats or observer rights, regular reporting, and consent rights over major decisions.
  4. A credible exit or income path. Dividends, refinancing, sale or listing, with a realistic timeline.
  5. Fit with the family's values and existing holdings. Many families favour sectors they know from their operating business, or apply sustainability and reputational screens.
  6. Clean counterparties. Every family office will run due diligence on you, including ownership, track record, sanctions and adverse media checks, similar to the KYB process payment providers use.

How to approach a family office

Cold, mass-mailed decks rarely work. Family offices are private, receive large volumes of unsolicited approaches and often do not publish contact details or mandates. Introductions through trusted advisers, existing investors or other families carry far more weight. Before any approach, prepare:

  • A short teaser and a full information memorandum
  • A financial model with clear assumptions and downside cases
  • A data room with corporate documents, contracts, permits and financial statements
  • Proposed terms: amount, instrument, valuation or pricing, governance and use of proceeds
  • Evidence of who else is committed or in discussion

Expect the process to take months. A family office may say yes quickly to a principal it trusts and slowly to everyone else. Be careful about marketing rules: in the UK, EU and US, promoting investments to private investors is regulated, and approaches are usually limited to professional investors through an authorised firm or under a specific exemption.

Common mistakes

  • Approaching a family office whose focus, ticket size or geography does not fit the deal
  • Inflated projections with no downside case
  • Unclear ownership or a cap table that changes during discussions
  • Asking for a commitment before sharing a data room
  • Treating the family office as passive money when it expects a say in key decisions
