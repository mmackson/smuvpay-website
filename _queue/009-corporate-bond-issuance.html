What a corporate bond is

A bond is a tradable loan. The company (the issuer) receives cash today and promises to pay investors interest, called the coupon, and to repay the principal on a set maturity date. Coupons can be fixed or floating, and maturities commonly run from three to ten years, sometimes longer for utilities and infrastructure. Because bonds are sold to many investors and can be traded, they can raise larger amounts and longer tenors than a single bank will usually lend, often with fewer restrictions on how the business is run.

The trade-off is disclosure, process and a minimum practical size. Public benchmark bonds in the euro and sterling markets are often several hundred million in size. Smaller companies tend to use private placements or smaller listed issues on growth markets instead.

Who is involved

  • Arrangers and bookrunners: investment banks or arrangers that structure the deal, run marketing and build the order book.
  • Legal counsel: separate lawyers for the issuer and the arrangers draft the documents and run due diligence.
  • Auditors: provide comfort letters on the financial information in the offering document.
  • Rating agencies: assign a credit rating if the issuer wants one. Many institutional investors need a rating to buy.
  • Trustee or agent: represents bondholders and enforces the terms if something goes wrong.
  • Paying agent and clearing systems: handle coupon and principal payments. International bonds usually settle through Euroclear or Clearstream.
  • Listing venue: for example the London Stock Exchange, Euronext Dublin or the Luxembourg Stock Exchange.

Corporate bond issuance step by step

  1. Define the need. Amount, currency, tenor and use of proceeds. Match the currency to your revenues where possible, or plan the hedge. Our guide to FX spreads shows how conversion costs add up over time.
  2. Choose the route. Public listed bond, private placement to a small group of institutions, or a structure such as a project or green bond. This decides the documents and investor base.
  3. Appoint the team. Mandate the arrangers, lawyers, trustee and agents. The mandate letter sets out roles and fees.
  4. Get rated, if needed. The rating process typically takes several weeks and includes management meetings with the agency.
  5. Due diligence and documentation. Lawyers review the business, contracts and financials. They draft the prospectus or offering circular, the terms and conditions, the trust deed or agency agreement, and the subscription agreement.
  6. Approval and listing. If the bond is listed on a regulated market, the prospectus is approved by the regulator, such as the FCA in the UK or the Central Bank of Ireland. Bonds sold in large minimum denominations, such as €100,000 under EU rules, are aimed at professional investors and need lighter disclosure than retail bonds.
  7. Marketing. A roadshow or investor calls, for debut issuers especially. Investors want to understand the credit story, the cash flows and the covenants.
  8. Bookbuilding and pricing. The bookrunners announce initial price guidance, collect orders, then tighten the price as demand builds. The final coupon is set as a spread over a benchmark, such as gilts or mid-swaps.
  9. Allocation and settlement. Bonds are allocated to investors and the issuer receives the cash on the settlement date, commonly around five business days after pricing for international bonds.

What investors look at

  • Leverage and interest cover, and how they change after the new debt
  • Stability and predictability of cash flows
  • Covenants: limits on further debt, asset sales, dividends and change of control
  • Ranking: senior or subordinated, secured or unsecured
  • Governance, ownership and the quality of reporting
  • For green, social or sustainability bonds, the framework and the reporting on use of proceeds

Costs and timeline

Costs fall into arranger fees, legal fees, rating fees, listing and agency fees, and the ongoing cost of reporting. Fees vary widely by deal size, market and complexity, so ask for a full fee schedule from each adviser at the start. A repeat issuer with an existing programme can price a new bond within days of deciding. A debut issuer should plan for three to six months, mostly spent on documentation, due diligence and rating.

After the deal

Issuance is the start of a relationship that lasts until maturity. The issuer pays coupons on time, publishes accounts and inside information as listing rules require, reports covenant compliance to the trustee, and keeps investors informed. Treasury operations matter here: coupon payments need to reach the paying agent on time and in the right currency, through reliable payment rails. Most issuers plan refinancing a year or more before maturity.

Common mistakes on a first issue

  • Starting documentation before the financial statements are audited and up to date
  • Agreeing tight covenants that block future plans
  • Choosing a currency with no natural hedge in the business
  • Underestimating the reporting work after listing
