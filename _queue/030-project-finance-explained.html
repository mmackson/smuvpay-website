How project finance works

The sponsors, the companies or investors promoting the project, set up a special purpose vehicle (SPV). The SPV signs the contracts to build and operate the asset, borrows the debt and holds the licences and land rights. Lenders can claim the SPV's assets and cash flows if things go wrong, but usually have no claim, or only a limited claim, on the sponsors themselves. That is why it is called non-recourse or limited-recourse finance.

The structure lets sponsors take on projects much larger than their own balance sheets could support, and keeps the risk of one project away from the rest of their business. The price is complexity: dozens of contracts, long due diligence, and lenders who control many decisions until the debt is repaid.

The capital stack

  • Senior debt: usually the largest piece, from commercial banks, development finance institutions, export credit agencies or bond investors. It is repaid first.
  • Mezzanine or subordinated debt: sometimes used to fill a gap, at a higher cost, repaid after senior debt.
  • Equity: from sponsors and co-investors such as infrastructure funds, pension funds and family offices. It takes the first loss and receives what is left after debt service.

The split depends on how predictable the cash flows are. Projects with long-term contracted revenue, such as a solar plant with a 20-year power purchase agreement, often carry debt of around 70% to 80% of total cost. Projects exposed to market prices or demand risk get less. The right figure varies by sector, country and lender appetite.

The contracts lenders rely on

  1. Offtake or concession agreement: who buys the output, at what price, for how long. This is the source of repayment.
  2. Construction (EPC) contract: usually fixed price and date-certain, with penalties for delay and underperformance.
  3. Operation and maintenance contract: who runs the asset once built, with performance guarantees.
  4. Supply contracts: fuel, feedstock or other inputs, where the project needs them.
  5. Land, permits and licences: the right to build and operate, with no unresolved challenges.
  6. Insurance: covering construction, operation, business interruption and, in some countries, political risk.

The guiding principle is that each risk sits with the party best able to manage it. Lenders test this in due diligence with technical, legal, insurance and market advisers.

How lenders size the debt

The main test is the debt service coverage ratio (DSCR): the cash available in a period divided by the interest and principal due in that period. Lenders set a minimum, often somewhere between 1.2x and 1.5x for contracted infrastructure and higher for riskier assets, and size the loan so the forecast never falls below it. Worked example: if a project is expected to generate £10 million a year of cash for debt service and lenders require a DSCR of 1.3x, annual debt service is capped at about £7.7 million. The loan amount follows from that figure, the interest rate and the tenor.

Other protections include reserve accounts that hold several months of debt service, cash sweeps when performance is weak, and restrictions on paying dividends to sponsors unless the ratios are met.

The stages of a project financing

  1. Development. Sponsors secure land, permits, studies and key contracts at their own risk. This is often the hardest money to find.
  2. Structuring and raising. Advisers build the financial model, prepare the information memorandum and approach lenders and equity investors.
  3. Due diligence and documentation. Lenders' advisers review everything. Term sheets become loan agreements, security documents and an intercreditor agreement.
  4. Financial close. All conditions are met, documents signed and funding becomes available. Drawdowns follow construction milestones.
  5. Construction. The riskiest phase. Delays and cost overruns are the main threats.
  6. Operation and refinancing. Once the asset is running and de-risked, sponsors often refinance on better terms, sometimes through a project bond.

From mandate to financial close, a large project commonly takes 12 to 24 months, and longer for first-of-a-kind technology or emerging markets.

What makes a project bankable

  • Creditworthy offtakers and contract terms longer than the debt
  • An experienced construction contractor and operator
  • Proven technology
  • Permits and land rights fully secured
  • Sponsors with a track record and real equity at risk
  • Currency risk addressed: debt in the same currency as revenue, or a hedge
  • Clear, legally enforceable security under local law
