What an institutional bond mandate is

The phrase is used in two ways. On the issuing side, a mandate is the appointment of arrangers and bookrunners to bring a bond to market. On the investing side, an institutional investor such as a pension fund or insurer gives an asset manager a mandate to run a bond portfolio within agreed guidelines. This guide focuses on the issuing side, where most of the decisions belong to the issuer's CFO and treasury team, and covers investment mandates briefly at the end. How issuers award a bond mandate

Frequent issuers keep a panel of banks and rotate roles between them. A first-time or occasional issuer usually runs a short selection process:

  1. Define the deal. Target amount, currency, tenor, ranking, use of proceeds, and whether the bond will be public, private or labelled green or social.
  2. Send a request for proposal. Ask a shortlist of banks or arrangers for their view on structure, pricing, investor demand, timetable and fees.
  3. Compare the pitches. Look past the headline price indication. Ask which investors they actually reach, their recent deals of similar size and sector, who will be on the team day to day, and how they would handle a weak market.
  4. Choose the roles. Decide how many banks to appoint and in which positions.
  5. Sign the mandate letter. Only then does the bank start formal work and announce the deal when the time comes.

Relationships matter. Banks that lend to the issuer often expect a role on its bonds, and issuers often reward lenders with mandates. That is legitimate, but the issuer should still test whether each bank adds distribution or expertise.

Roles on a bond deal

  • Arranger or global coordinator: leads structuring, coordinates the other banks and the timetable, and usually sits closest to the issuer.
  • Active bookrunners: market the bond, take orders, build the book and recommend pricing and allocation.
  • Passive bookrunners or co-managers: smaller roles, often given to relationship banks, with less involvement in pricing and distribution.
  • Other advisers: an independent debt adviser can sit on the issuer's side, helping it select banks and challenge their recommendations.

Two to four active bookrunners is common on a mid-sized benchmark deal. More banks add reach, but also cost and coordination effort, and can lead to duplicate orders that inflate the book.

What the mandate letter covers

  • Roles, titles and the order in which banks are named
  • Indicative terms of the bond, usually non-binding
  • Fees: often a percentage of the amount raised, split between banks by role, plus expenses
  • Exclusivity: how long the issuer agrees not to appoint others for the same deal
  • Indemnities from the issuer to the banks
  • Termination rights, and any fee payable if the deal does not go ahead
  • A tail clause, which can entitle the bank to fees if the issuer completes a similar deal within a set period after ending the mandate
  • Confidentiality and handling of inside information

The mandate letter is normally not a commitment to buy the bonds. Most institutional bonds are sold on a best-efforts basis until pricing, when the banks sign a subscription agreement. Read the termination and tail clauses carefully. They decide what you owe if plans change.

How a mandate is run

  1. Kick-off. Agree the timetable, document list, workstreams and weekly calls. One person on the issuer side should own the timetable.
  2. Documentation and due diligence. Lawyers draft, auditors prepare comfort letters, the rating process runs in parallel.
  3. Investor work. Presentation, roadshow or calls. Banks report investor feedback in writing.
  4. Launch and bookbuilding. Price guidance is announced, orders come in, the book is updated in real time and guidance is tightened as demand allows.
  5. Pricing and allocation. The issuer agrees the final terms and approves allocations. Ask for a written allocation policy in advance, so long-term investors are favoured over short-term traders where that is the goal.
  6. Settlement and debrief. Funds arrive on the settlement date. A debrief with order book data helps with the next deal.

The issuer's own operations must be ready too: settlement accounts, currency conversion and the later coupon payments. Moving large sums in several currencies is easier with a clear plan for multi-currency treasury.

Investment mandates: the other side

When a pension fund, insurer or family office hands a bond portfolio to an asset manager, the investment mandate sets the benchmark, permitted instruments, credit quality limits, duration range, currency rules and reporting. Managers are usually selected through a request for proposal and reviewed regularly against the benchmark. Issuers benefit from understanding these mandates: they decide which investors can buy a bond at all.

Common mistakes

  • Choosing banks on the tightest price indication alone
  • Appointing too many bookrunners
  • Signing broad exclusivity or tail clauses without limits
  • No single owner of the timetable on the issuer side
