When multi-currency treasury management becomes necessary

A company that sells only in sterling and pays only in sterling has a cash problem, not a currency problem. That changes once it has a US customer, a Polish development team or a supplier in China. Each foreign flow adds exchange rate risk, conversion cost and timing risk. A few months later, finance is often converting money at the bank's rate whenever a payment falls due, with no view of what that costs.

Common signs it is time for a deliberate approach:

  • More than 10% to 20% of revenue or costs are in foreign currencies.
  • Monthly results swing because of exchange rates, not trading.
  • You convert money in, then convert it back out a few weeks later.
  • Nobody can say what the business paid in FX spreads last year.

Step 1: map your currency flows

List every currency you receive and pay, with monthly amounts and timing. Separate the predictable (payroll, rent, subscriptions) from the variable (supplier invoices, customer receipts). Then net them.

Worked example: a UK software company receives €400,000 a month from European customers and pays €150,000 to a team in Spain. It also pays $80,000 for cloud hosting and receives $30,000 from US customers. If it converts every receipt to sterling and buys every payment back, it converts about €550,000 and $110,000 a month. If it pays euro and dollar costs from euro and dollar balances, it only needs to convert the net amounts: €250,000 into sterling and $50,000 out of sterling. That cuts converted volume by more than half, and the FX spread paid falls with it.

Step 2: set balance targets per currency

For each currency, decide how much to hold:

  • Operating balance: enough to cover the next one to three months of known payments in that currency.
  • Buffer: extra headroom for late receipts or unexpected costs.
  • Sweep rule: anything above the target is converted to the home currency, or moved to where it earns interest.

Holding too much foreign cash leaves profits exposed to rate moves. Holding too little forces rushed conversions. A written rule ("convert surplus euros above €300,000 every Friday") removes guesswork and makes treasury decisions easy to audit.

Step 3: choose an account structure

  • Multi-currency accounts: one account holding balances in several currencies, often with local account details so you can receive euros over SEPA, sterling over Faster Payments and dollars over ACH.
  • Virtual accounts: virtual IBANs per entity, customer or currency feed one master account and make reconciliation simpler.
  • Local bank accounts: sometimes needed for tax, payroll or regulatory reasons in a country, at the cost of more banking relationships to run.
  • More than one provider: a second banking route protects you if a bank exits a corridor or closes the account. Our guide to correspondent banking and de-risking explains why this happens.

If client or customer funds are involved, check how each provider safeguards them and whether balances count as bank deposits.

Step 4: manage FX risk

There are three kinds of FX exposure to think about:

  • Transaction risk: the rate moves between agreeing a price and paying or getting paid.
  • Translation risk: foreign subsidiaries' results change value when reported in the home currency.
  • Economic risk: a long-term currency shift makes your products dearer or cheaper than a competitor's.

Growing businesses usually start with transaction risk. The simplest tools are natural hedging, which means matching income and costs in the same currency, and forward contracts, which fix a rate today for a payment on a future date. Many companies hedge a share of forecast flows, for example 50% to 80% of the next six to twelve months, rather than all of it, because forecasts change. Options give protection with the chance to benefit from a good move, in exchange for an upfront premium. A written hedging policy should state what you hedge, how far ahead, with which instruments and who approves trades.

Step 5: control and report

  1. Daily visibility: one view of balances across all currencies and accounts.
  2. Cash forecast: 13 weeks is a common horizon, by currency.
  3. Payment controls: approval limits and maker-checker sign-off on payments and FX trades.
  4. Cost reporting: track spreads and fees per provider each month against the mid-market rate.
  5. Reconciliation: match every receipt and payment, as covered in our guide to payment reconciliation.

Where stablecoins fit

Some businesses hold part of their working capital in dollar stablecoins to move money between entities outside banking hours or to pay into corridors where bank rails are slow. Treat them as another currency balance with its own target, custody arrangements and conversion costs.
