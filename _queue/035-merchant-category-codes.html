What a merchant category code is

A merchant category code is a four-digit number assigned to every merchant account that accepts cards. The codes follow the ISO 18245 standard, and Visa and Mastercard each publish their own lists that broadly align with it. Some well-known examples:

  • 5411 Grocery stores and supermarkets
  • 5812 Restaurants
  • 5814 Fast food restaurants
  • 4111 Local and suburban commuter transport
  • 5967 Direct marketing, inbound telemarketing
  • 7995 Betting, including lottery tickets and casino gaming chips
  • 6051 Non-financial institutions, including foreign currency, money orders and some crypto purchases

The code travels in the authorisation message for every transaction, so the issuer knows the type of merchant before deciding whether to approve the payment.

Who assigns your MCC

Your acquirer assigns the code at onboarding, based on how you describe your business and what its underwriters see on your website and documents. If you sell through a payment facilitator, the payfac assigns it for your sub-merchant account. Our guide to PayFacs and ISOs explains that model.

The code should describe your main line of business. If you run two very different businesses, such as a shop and a travel agency, you may need two merchant accounts with separate codes.

How your MCC affects fees and interchange

In some markets, interchange tables include category-specific rates. In the US, for example, schemes publish lower rates for some categories such as supermarkets, utilities or charities. In the UK and EEA, consumer interchange is capped regardless of category, so the effect there is smaller. Our guide to interchange fees explains how rates are set.

Acquirers also price by category. A code associated with high dispute rates will usually carry a higher markup, a reserve or both.

Approval rates

Issuers use the MCC in their fraud models. A payment at a category with high fraud may face stricter checks. Some issuers also block whole categories by policy, for example gambling codes on certain debit cards, and many UK banks let customers block gambling transactions themselves. A wrong code can trigger these blocks on a business that should not be affected. See how to raise your approval rate for other causes of declines.

Risk and monitoring

The schemes treat some codes as high-risk, and acquirers must register merchants in them with the scheme and monitor them more closely. Examples include betting (7995), dating and escort services (7273), direct marketing categories such as 5966 and 5967, and some financial services codes. Exact lists and requirements differ between Visa and Mastercard and change over time. Being in one of these categories is not a problem in itself, but it affects pricing, reserves and the number of acquirers willing to board you. Our high-risk merchant account guide covers what to expect.

Customer rewards and corporate card rules

Card issuers use MCCs to decide rewards, such as extra points on travel or dining. Corporate card programmes often restrict employees to specific categories. A code that does not match your business can stop a business customer's card from working or deny a consumer the rewards they expect, which leads to complaints and sometimes disputes.

The risks of a wrong or misleading MCC

Using a code that hides what you sell is called miscoding. Schemes treat deliberate miscoding as a serious breach, particularly when it disguises a high-risk activity such as gambling as something lower risk. The consequences can include scheme fines for the acquirer, termination of the merchant account and, for the worst cases, a listing on the Mastercard MATCH list. See why merchant accounts get terminated for how that works.

Honest miscoding also happens. Your business may have changed since onboarding, or the acquirer chose a generic code. Either way, it is worth checking.

A worked example

A UK company sells online courses and launched with a generic code for miscellaneous business services. Corporate customers report that their company cards are declined, because their programmes only allow education and training categories. The company asks its acquirer to review the code, explains its business with evidence, and the acquirer moves it to an education code. Corporate card declines drop, and the change is properly recorded with the scheme.

How to check and fix your MCC

  1. Find your code. It often appears in your acquirer dashboard or merchant agreement. If not, ask your account manager.
  2. Compare it to what you sell. Look up the code's description in the scheme lists your acquirer can share.
  3. Request a change in writing if it is wrong, with a short description of your business and evidence such as your website and product range.
  4. Tell your acquirer before you change your model. Adding a new product line in a different category may need a new code or a new account.
  5. Watch your data after the change. Compare approvals, fees and disputes before and after.
