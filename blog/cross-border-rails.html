Cross-border payments

Why cross-border rails decide
your cost — not your provider.

20 Nov 20264 min readCross-border payments

Most teams compare payment providers by the headline fee — a percentage, a flat rate, maybe an FX markup. But the invoice fee is only a fraction of the real cost of a cross-border payment. The rest is decided by the rails and intermediaries your money actually travels through.

The cost isn't the fee — it's the route

When you send money across a border, it doesn't move in a straight line. It moves through a chain: your bank or provider, possibly a correspondent bank, a clearing or settlement network, an FX conversion, and a receiving institution. Every hop adds cost, time and a point of failure.

Two payments to the same country, for the same amount, on the same day can cost very different amounts — purely because they took different routes. That is why "cheapest provider" is the wrong question. The right question is: which rail does this corridor support, and what does that rail actually cost end-to-end?

The rails available today

  • Correspondent banking (SWIFT) — global reach, but multiple intermediaries, slower settlement, and opaque fees. Best for large or non-standard corridors.
  • Local rails — schemes like SEPA, Faster Payments, ACH or real-time domestic networks. Fast and cheap, but only when both ends sit in the same scheme.
  • Card rails — instant authorisation but expensive at scale; built for checkout, not treasury.
  • Stablecoins — can settle in minutes across borders, but bring their own on/off-ramp, custody and compliance considerations.

How to choose, corridor by corridor

There is no single best rail. The right answer depends on the corridor, the amount, how fast it must arrive, and the currencies involved. A payment of £5,000 to a supplier in France is a different problem from a $500 payout to a contractor in a market with thin banking coverage.

Smart routing means matching each payment to the rail that optimises for what matters on that flow — cost, speed, or reliability — rather than forcing everything through one provider.

What to ask your provider

  1. Which rails does this corridor actually use — not just "we support 150 countries"?
  2. How many intermediaries sit in the chain, and what do they each charge?
  3. What is the true FX spread, not the advertised markup?
  4. What happens when a payment fails or is held — how fast do you find out, and what does recovery cost?

Cross-border payments are an infrastructure problem before they are a pricing problem. Get the rails right, and the cost — and the risk — usually follows.
