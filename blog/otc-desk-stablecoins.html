How an OTC desk works for
large stablecoin trades.

21 Nov 20265 min readTreasury & digital assets

Placing a large order on a public exchange moves the price against you before it fills. Over-the-counter (OTC) desks exist so businesses, funds and high-net-worth clients can buy or sell size at one agreed price, without showing the order to the market.

Why size changes the trade

An exchange order book only holds so much liquidity at each price. A $2 million market order can eat through several price levels, and the average fill ends up well away from the price you saw. This is slippage. An OTC desk sources liquidity from many venues and its own inventory, and quotes one all-in price for the full amount.

How a trade happens

  1. Request for quote (RFQ): you tell the desk the asset, side, amount and settlement currency, for example "buy 1,500,000 USDT against EUR".
  2. Firm quote: the desk returns a price valid for a short window, often seconds to a few minutes.
  3. Acceptance: you confirm, and the price is locked.
  4. Settlement: one side sends funds, then the other side delivers. Depending on the relationship this happens with pre-funding, at the same time through escrow, or on agreed credit terms.

Where the cost sits

OTC desks rarely charge a visible fee. They earn the spread between their quote and where they can cover the trade. At size, a tight OTC spread is usually far cheaper than exchange slippage plus trading fees. Ask for the quote against a reference price so you can compare desks fairly.

Common business uses

  • Converting stablecoin revenue into EUR, GBP or USD for payroll and suppliers.
  • Funding stablecoin payouts in markets where banking is slow or expensive.
  • Moving treasury balances between fiat and digital assets.
  • Settling cross-border trade where local currency is hard to source, sometimes with face-to-face settlement where it is lawful.

What to check before you trade

  • Onboarding: a serious desk runs full KYC or KYB and source-of-funds checks. If it does not ask, walk away.
  • Regulatory status: who holds the licence or registration in your jurisdiction. Our regulatory guide explains what to look for.
  • Settlement risk: who sends first, which wallets and bank accounts are used, and what happens if the other side fails to deliver.
  • Wallet screening: blockchain analytics on incoming and outgoing addresses, so you never receive funds linked to sanctioned or illicit sources.
