Merchant acquiring

Rolling reserves explained:
why processors hold your money.

21 Nov 20264 min readMerchant acquiring

A rolling reserve is the part of your sales a processor holds back to cover future chargebacks and refunds. For new and high-risk merchants it is one of the most common account terms, and one of the least understood until the cash-flow impact appears.

How a rolling reserve works

The processor keeps a fixed percentage of each day's settlement, commonly 5% to 10%, and releases each day's held amount after a set period, often 90 to 180 days. After the first hold period, money is released every day at the same pace it is held, so the reserve stops growing and stays at a roughly constant balance.

Example: a merchant settling $300,000 a month with a 10% reserve held for 180 days has about $180,000 locked up at any time once the reserve is fully built. That is working capital the business cannot use.

Other reserve types

  • Upfront (capped) reserve: a fixed amount deposited or withheld at the start, then left in place.
  • Minimum reserve: a floor balance the account must always hold.
  • Delayed settlement: all funds held for a few extra days instead of a percentage held for months.

Why processors require it

When a customer disputes a charge months after the sale, the acquirer pays the issuer first and recovers the money from the merchant. If the merchant has closed, run out of cash or disappeared, the acquirer takes the loss. A reserve covers that gap. Processors size it using your industry, refund and chargeback history, delivery times, average ticket size and how long you have been trading.

How to negotiate it down

  1. Bring processing history. Six to twelve months of statements with low chargeback ratios is the strongest argument you have.
  2. Shorten your exposure window. Faster delivery and no long pre-orders reduce how long a sale can turn into a dispute.
  3. Offer a cap. Ask for the reserve to stop at a fixed amount instead of rolling forever.
  4. Agree a review date. Put a 90- or 180-day review into the contract, with clear metrics for reducing or removing the reserve.
  5. Use more than one acquirer. Spreading volume lowers each provider's exposure and makes each reserve smaller.

A reserve is not a penalty. It is how acquirers say yes to businesses they would otherwise decline. For high-risk merchants, the goal is to start with workable terms and earn better ones with clean data. See the industries we support.
